# Mapping the microverse and modelling its drivers

**Speaker:** Bas Dutilh, Friedrich Schiller University Jena (Germany)

**Session 9** of the first edition of the School of Artificial Intelligence Applied to Microbiomes: Friday 3 October 2025, 09:30–12:30, a talk with assignments on the Microverse Mapper web application.

## Summary

*The 2025 programme published no abstracts. This summary was written for the archive from the slides.*

The microverse is "the globally connected network of microbiomes that covers the Earth and all things on it". Placing 22,518 metagenomes from 140 biomes on one map (von Meijenfeldt, Hogeweg and Dutilh, Nature Ecology & Evolution 2023) separates host-associated, soil and freshwater, and marine microbiomes, and shows that bacteria are distributed unevenly across it. The social niche breadth of a taxon, how varied the communities it lives in are, sorts microbes from generalists to specialists, at every taxonomic rank. Generalists often outcompete specialists locally, vary across communities and are fast-growing opportunists, as growth rates predicted from codon usage bias show; their genomes are not larger, but their pan-genomes are larger and more open. Specialists are less abundant but ecologically and evolutionarily stable. Rich nutrient sources such as plant roots attract fast growers with competitive functions.

## What the session covered

The talk alternated with assignments on the Microverse Mapper, a web application of the speaker's group that places microbiome samples on the map:

1. Read the Help page of the Microverse Mapper.
2. For the genus *Roseobacter*: in how many samples and how many biomes it is found (at a cut-off of 100 reads), in which biome it is typical, which ten genera are most abundant in marine samples that contain it, and which of them are generalists and which specialists.
3. Which oral samples stand out on the map, why, and what that says about the data; how many types of bioreactor there are.
4. The taxonomic lineage of *Roseobacter*, and in how many samples and biomes each rank is found.
5. How generalists become so successful, and how specialists avoid being outcompeted.
6. Take microbiome datasets (your own, or from [MGnify](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metagenomics)), convert them to [BIOM format](http://biom-format.org/documentation/biom_format.html), upload them to the Microverse Mapper, and check where they fall, from how many studies their 100 closest samples come, and which publications describe them.
